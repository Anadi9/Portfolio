To comply with India's DPDP Act, your app needs to: show an itemised privacy
notice, take separate consent for each purpose, let people withdraw consent,
access, correct and delete their data, erase data you no longer need, protect it
with reasonable security and a year of access logs, report breaches within 72
hours, and get parental consent for users under 18. Most of this applies from
around 13 May 2027.

This page turns that into a build list. It describes what the law asks a
product to do; it isn't legal advice, and your lawyer decides what your policy
says.

## When does the DPDP Act apply?

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act was passed in 2023. The DPDP Rules that
make it work were notified in November 2025, with a phased start:

| When | What starts |
| --- | --- |
| November 2025 | The Data Protection Board is set up |
| Around November 2026 | Consent Manager provisions, about 12 months after notification |
| Around 13 May 2027 | The core obligations: notice, consent, security, breach reporting, user rights, retention |

It applies to digital personal data processed in India, and to processing
outside India when it's connected to offering goods or services to people in
India.

## The DPDP checklist, as product work

### 1. Notice

- [ ] Before or when you collect personal data, show a notice that stands on its
  own (not buried in your terms).
- [ ] It lists each piece of data and the purpose for each, in plain language.
- [ ] It tells people how to withdraw consent and how to complain.

### 2. Consent

- [ ] Consent is a clear action (an unticked box they tick, a button they press).
- [ ] Each purpose is consented to separately: "create my account" and "send me
  offers" are two choices, not one.
- [ ] You keep a record: who, when, which purposes, which version of the notice.

### 3. Withdrawal

- [ ] Withdrawing is as easy as consenting was, inside the product.
- [ ] Withdrawal actually stops the processing for that purpose, including in the
  tools you send data to.

### 4. User rights

- [ ] People can get a summary of the data you hold and who you shared it with.
- [ ] They can correct and update it.
- [ ] They can ask for erasure, and deletion reaches your database, your backups
  on their normal cycle, and your vendors.
- [ ] You publish a contact for privacy questions and answer within the time the
  Rules set.

### 5. Retention

- [ ] Every kind of personal data has a retention period tied to its purpose.
- [ ] Erasure runs on a schedule, not by hand, with notice before inactive
  accounts are removed where the Rules require it.

### 6. Security

- [ ] Personal data is encrypted, masked or otherwise protected.
- [ ] Access is limited to the people and services that need it.
- [ ] Access logs are kept for at least a year.

Failing to take reasonable security safeguards carries the Act's largest
penalty, up to ₹250 crore, so this is not the item to leave for last.

### 7. Breaches

- [ ] You'd notice a breach: alerting on unusual access, not a customer email.
- [ ] You can tell affected people without delay.
- [ ] You can send the Data Protection Board a detailed report within 72 hours.

### 8. Children

- [ ] You know whether users under 18 can sign up.
- [ ] If they can, you get verifiable consent from a parent first.
- [ ] No tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted ads aimed at children.

### 9. Vendors

- [ ] You have a list of every tool that receives personal data: analytics,
  email, payments, support chat, AI APIs.
- [ ] Each has a contract that covers what it may do with the data and how it
  deletes it.

## Where do apps usually fall short?

The same four gaps come up again and again:

1. **One checkbox for everything**, usually "I agree to the terms and privacy
   policy". That's not specific consent.
2. **Account deletion that only hides the account**, while the data stays in the
   database, the email tool and the analytics platform.
3. **No access logs**, so a breach can't be investigated, let alone reported in
   72 hours.
4. **Apps built fast with AI tools** that never had a privacy pass at all,
   often with database rules that let anyone read user tables.
